Nature · Ascea, Campania, Italy

Loggerhead turtles keep nesting further west, and Italy's beach clubs are learning to share the sand.

Loggerheads that for decades nested almost only in Greece, Türkiye and Cyprus now come ashore along Italy, France and Spain, where the first confirmed Spanish nest was found in 2001 and nesting has been annual since 2014. Sandra Hochscheid of the Stazione Zoologica Anton Dohrn follows nesting females by satellite, and Carlos Carreras at the University of Barcelona has traced the newcomers' genomes back to eastern Mediterranean and Caribbean populations; a study in Campania found 87 percent of nests in 2022 and 2023 lay within reach of beachfront lighting, and 82 percent needed someone to step in so the hatchlings went seaward rather than inland. Owners of the lidi who once brushed the tracks away now telephone them in, and Ascea, which has already dimmed its promenade, will require low-impact lighting on every beach concession from 2027.

Small Wonders · Leipzig, Germany

Thirty-one pet dogs have been taught to find invasive plants by smell.

Between 2021 and 2023 the Helmholtz Centre for Environmental Research in Leipzig trained 31 volunteers and their own dogs to detect Himalayan balsam, common ragweed and the knotweeds, three of the plants spreading fastest across Europe. Twelve teams sat the final test outdoors and eleven passed, finding the target plants about 75 percent of the time; Annegret Grimm-Seyfarth and colleagues published the results on November 4 in PLOS One. They also set down what such a scheme needs — how to choose handlers, how long to train, how closely to watch the first searches — because, as one of the authors put it, Enthusiasm is a great starting point, but reliable monitoring requires more than a good nose.

Quiet Kindness · Berri, South Australia

A Riverland town cooks lunch together every week, and the group runs itself.

Cooking Outside the Box meets weekly at the Berri Community Centre in South Australia's Riverland, where as many as 20 people cook a meal on a budget, often from fruit and vegetables picked in the centre's garden that morning. The not-for-profit ac.care set it up with Foodbank's help, though coordinator Sue Dayman says we don't really run it; basically, the group runs themselves — one member added a Zumba session to fill the time while the food is in the oven. Janet Becker, who joined a year ago, says it got her cooking at home again, and the group once made enough to feed itself and the people sleeping outside the centre.

Everyday Joy · Accra, Ghana

A photographer who opened his Accra studio in 1953 is being honoured at 97.

James Barnor, born in 1929, has been given the Outstanding Contribution to Photography award by the World Photography Organisation, and more than 50 of his prints go on show at Somerset House in London next April. He became the Daily Graphic's first staff photographer at 21, opened the Ever Young studio in Jamestown in 1953, photographed Kwame Nkrumah declaring Ghana's independence in 1957, spent the 1960s in London making portraits of Black Londoners for Drum magazine, then went home in 1970 to help set up the country's first colour-processing laboratory. His pictures hang in the Tate, the National Portrait Gallery and the Centre Pompidou; of the work he said, My camera became my companion, and I have never regretted it.

Science & Medicine · Boston, Massachusetts, USA

A single iron infusion let people with the commonest heart failure walk further.

Sixty-five people who had heart failure with preserved ejection fraction — the most common form, and the one with fewest treatments — together with iron deficiency were randomly given either one 1,000 mg infusion of ferric derisomaltose or a placebo. Twelve weeks later the iron group's peak oxygen uptake had risen about 10 percent while the placebo group's had not moved; they also covered more ground in a six-minute walk and moved up the scale doctors use to grade how much their symptoms limit them. Gregory Lewis and Rajeev Malhotra of Mass General Brigham published the trial in Circulation; it is small, the group was mostly white, self-reported health scores did not separate, and no serious harm was traced to the iron.

Global Progress · Pará and Rondônia, Brazil

Amazon cacao grew by a third, nearly all of it on land already cleared.

Embrapa's new mapping puts cacao in Pará at 116,400 hectares in 2024, up from about 87,300 in 2022, with 96 percent of it standing on ground cleared before 2020 — 98 percent in Rondônia, surveyed here for the first time at 5,340 hectares across 51 municipalities. Adriano Venturieri led the work, with field teams from CEPLAC and Emater checking it on the ground; most growers are smallholders, 84.6 percent of them farming five hectares or less, and about 11,444 hectares still read as forest from the air because the trees were planted under a thinned canopy. The figures start to matter on December 30, when the European Union's deforestation rules reach large traders, and Brazil's national plan asks for another 120,000 hectares grown this way by 2030.

Everyday Joy · Hull, England

A man who started walking in 1998 has reached his mother's front door.

Karl Bushby, 57, walked about 50,000 kilometres from the southern tip of Chile without once using motorised transport, and finished by crossing the Humber Bridge.

Small Wonders · 22 countries across Europe

Camera traps in 22 countries have made one shared album of Europe's wildlife.

Snapshot Europe released about 100,000 observations of mammals and birds, gathered by more than 160 camera-trap specialists, and made every image public.

Quiet Kindness · Castlemilk, Glasgow, Scotland

Castlemilk has a shop selling fresh food again, and it is not there to make money.

A not-for-profit community shop sells fresh produce and household essentials at up to 30 percent below the nearest supermarket, shaped by the residents who use it.
