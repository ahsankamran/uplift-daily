Nature · Grayland, Washington, USA

Snowy plovers have passed 3,000 birds on the Pacific coast.

The 2025 breeding survey counted more than 3,000 western snowy plovers along the US Pacific coast, the first time the figure written into the federal recovery plan has been reached, and preliminary Fish and Wildlife Service numbers now put it above 3,300. Washington's share came to 156 birds, the level the state calls healthy, after decades of work by state biologists including Allison Anholt and Josh Emm, the Shoalwater Bay Indian Tribe and others who find nests, band chicks and put up signs on beaches such as Grayland. The state has to hold that number for five years before the bird's endangered listing can be revisited, and chicks still fledge too slowly in some stretches, so Anholt expects the plovers will need looking after for a long time yet.

Everyday Joy · Chimborazo, Ecuador

Two friends carried a chess set to the top of Chimborazo.

Adam Wininger and Joshua Host played a game on the 20,593-foot summit of Chimborazo in March, and Guinness World Records has now confirmed it as the highest-altitude board game on record. The two, best friends aged 32 and 44, had turned back from an earlier attempt on the mountain when the weather closed in. Their mark beats the previous one — set by Tim Maw and Neil Laughton on Putha Hiunchuli in Nepal in 2024 — by 200 feet, and Wininger says that if anyone takes it from them they will simply go 200 feet higher again.

Quiet Kindness · Capel Sound, Victoria, Australia

A knitting group south of Melbourne has made more than a thousand garments this year.

The group that meets on Wednesday mornings at the Capel Sound Community Hub has knitted more than 1,000 baby clothes, toys, scarves, beanies and blankets in 2026, each item made to whatever the receiving charity asked for. It has been going over twenty years — starting at Nola's Wool Shop in Rosebud, then the Rosebud Community House, and for the past fifteen years the hub — sending work to Rosebud Hospital, Knit One Give One, Beanies for African Children and the Lions Club, and crocheting red poppies for Remembrance Day. Members say the company is half the point; the group is now short of wool, which can be left at the hub's front desk, and new knitters are welcome whether or not they can already knit.

Small Wonders · Medog County, Xizang, China

A Himalayan fern spent nearly two centuries under the wrong name.

Botanists at the Xishuangbanna Tropical Botanical Garden have described Drynaria lineatisora, a fern first collected in Myanmar and Vietnam in the nineteenth century and filed ever since as the widespread Drynaria coronans. Its spore clusters run in continuous lines up to 20 millimetres long, its scales are dark brown rather than pale yellow and its spores carry fine spines; 84 chloroplast genes put it on a branch of its own. Fieldwork in Medog County, on the southern flank of the Himalaya, turned up hundreds of plants growing on tree trunks between 840 and 2,400 metres, every one of them carrying the same long sori.

Science & Medicine · A trial in 11 countries

Pausing to reassess lung cancer patients sent more of them to curative surgery.

The MDT-BRIDGE trial enrolled 142 people with stage IIB to IIIB non-small-cell lung cancer at 43 centres in 11 countries, gave them two cycles of chemotherapy with durvalumab, then had a multidisciplinary team decide the next step rather than fixing the route at the outset. After that review 84.5% were judged fit for surgery, including 38 of the 50 whose tumours had been called borderline; 106 people were operated on, 96.2% of those operations took the tumour out completely, and 92.3% of the whole group went on to treatment given with the intention of curing them. The work, led from McGill University and the LungenClinic in Grosshansdorf and published in Nature Medicine, also found that patients whose circulating tumour DNA cleared early did better, which may in time show who can be spared an operation altogether.

Global Progress · Worldwide

Measles deaths have fallen to the lowest figure ever recorded.

An estimated 72,000 people died of measles in 2025, a 91% fall since 2000 and the smallest annual toll yet counted, according to a World Health Organization report published today. The same report puts the number of lives saved by measles vaccination over those 25 years at more than 60 million. Coverage remains lopsided — 84% of children worldwide had a first dose and 77% a second, against 69% and 57% in low-income countries — which leaves 29 million children unvaccinated or only partly protected, and is where the next campaigns are to be aimed.
