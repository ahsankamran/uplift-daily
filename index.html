Small Wonders · University of Bonn, Germany

Jumping spiders have two kinds of sleep, and in one of them their eyes move.

Daniela Rössler and Nadja Geiger at the University of Bonn filmed the jumping spider Portia fimbriata through the night: at sunset it spins a small silk platform, hangs upside down from it, grooms itself and drifts off, then cycles between a still, quiet state and a second one in which its legs curl, its body twitches and its eyes move. Spiders in that second state took an average of 4.27 seconds to respond to a loud noise, against 2.71 seconds when merely quiescent and 1.69 seconds when awake — the measurements were published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences on October 26. The line that led to spiders split from the line that led to us roughly 500 million years ago, and the Bonn group now wants to compare sleep across spider species, and in the wild rather than the lab.

Nature · Ile-Balkhash State Nature Reserve, Kazakhstan

A second tiger is loose in the Ili delta, where none has lived for 70 years.

Kazakhstan's ecology ministry let a male Amur tiger, named Amur, out into the Ile-Balkhash State Nature Reserve on October 31, three months after a female called Umit walked out of her enclosure on July 31. Both wear satellite collars; they came from a group of four tigers brought from Russia's Khabarovsk Territory in May, and the reserve has been laying in their food first — 30 kulans were moved across from Altyn-Emel national park in September, along with Bukhara deer. Two cubs are still under supervision and will go out when keepers judge them ready; the aim, set with the Amur Tiger Centre and WWF, is a population in the Ili delta and southern Balkhash that breeds without help.

Global Progress · Pantanal Matogrossense National Park, Brazil

Brazil's Pantanal national park is about a third larger than it was.

Brazil has added roughly 48,000 hectares — about 185 square miles — of wetland and riverside forest to Pantanal Matogrossense National Park, taking it from some 135,000 hectares to 183,095. The Chico Mendes Institute for Biodiversity Conservation, which runs the park, chose the new ground along the corridors animals already use to move in and out, and is working with neighbouring landowners on buffer zones either side of them. The park sits in the world's largest flooded grassland, counted home to 463 bird species, 269 kinds of fish and the densest population of jaguars anywhere, along with giant otters, giant armadillos and marsh deer.

Quiet Kindness · Melbourne, Victoria, Australia

A Melbourne man has given blood at all 85 donation centres in Australia.

Trevor Bergman, 59, finished a decade-long project in May with a plasma donation in Canberra — the last of Lifeblood's 85 centres, and his 545th donation since he first rolled up a sleeve at 18. The idea came to him on a holiday in Perth, and he worked up the east coast, across to Western Australia and north to Darwin; turned away in Albany with a cold, he said he did not mind, because “the security of the blood supply — you've got to make sure that's as good as it can be.” He is one of about 150 Australians carrying the anti-D antibody, which Lifeblood gives to nearly 48,000 pregnant women a year to keep their immune systems from attacking their babies' blood.

Everyday Joy · Kunsthalle Bremen, Bremen, Germany

A German museum has given eight galleries over to the dachshund.

“The Dachshund: An Icon Off the Leash” has opened at the Kunsthalle Bremen with about 120 works, among them Picasso's reworkings of Velázquez's Las Meninas with his dachshund Lump in them, and David Hockney's 1995 paintings of his own two. Annett Reckert curated it; the first room holds Thorsten Brinkmann's installation Toxie Love, built partly from objects borrowed from the Dachshund Museum in Regensburg, and the walls also carry Warhol, Otto Dix and Max Liebermann. On Thursdays and Saturdays three of the eight rooms are open to dogs about a foot or less at the shoulder, who book a slot online and check in at a Dachshund Lounge; the show runs to March 28, 2027.

Science & Medicine · King's College London, England

Twelve short online sessions eased anxiety in pregnancy and after the birth.

A trial led by Colette Hirsch at King's College London randomised 268 pregnant people between 16 and 28 weeks — all of them prone to repetitive worry, none carrying a psychiatric diagnosis — either to usual maternity care or to usual care plus RELAX, a self-led online course of 12 short sessions spread over four weeks. Each session puts about 30 everyday situations to the user and trains them to read the ambiguous ones more generously; 81.3 percent of the RELAX group completed at least ten of the twelve on a laptop or tablet at home. Anxiety, depression and repetitive negative thinking were all lower than in the usual-care group, both before and after the birth, in results published in JAMA Network Open on November 4.

Nature · River Ness catchment, Highland, Scotland

Beavers are going back into the waters around Loch Ness after 500 years.

NatureScot licensed the release of up to 15 beaver families into the River Ness catchment over three years, five centuries after the last sighting at the loch.

Small Wonders · University of Tübingen, Germany

Two crows called Iggy and Lemmy decide what they are looking at twice as fast as we do.

Over thousands of touchscreen trials the pair averaged 235 milliseconds a decision, roughly half what monkeys and people need, with no loss of accuracy.

Global Progress · Desa'a forest, Tigray, Ethiopia

A dry forest above Ethiopia's Rift Valley has been named a UN restoration flagship.

More than 23,000 hectares of degraded escarpment are being brought back, holding some 500,000 tonnes of soil in place and catching 1.9 million cubic metres of rain a year.
