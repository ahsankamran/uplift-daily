Everyday Joy · Huinchiri, Cusco Region, Peru

Every June for nearly 600 years, four Quechua communities in Peru's Andes dismantle and completely rebuild the world's last handwoven Inca rope bridge across the Apurímac gorge.

The Q'eswachaka suspension bridge — thirty metres long, suspended twenty-eight metres above the Apurímac River canyon — is cut to the river below each June and rewoven from scratch over four days by around 1,000 people from the communities of Quehue, Choccayhua, Chaupibanda, and Huinchiri. Women harvest and twist ichu grass on the slopes above the gorge; men braid the cords into six main cables each thicker than a human torso; and master bridge-builders known as Chakacamayoc work from opposite canyon walls until their sections meet in the middle — the only measurement tool being generations of embedded craft knowledge. UNESCO inscribed the tradition as Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2013, recognising it as a continuous practice unchanged since Inca times; the 2026 reconstruction ran June 11–13, and the bridge will carry foot traffic across the gorge until next June's ceremony dismantles it again.

Global Progress · Global Oceans

On World Oceans Day, the UN confirmed that just over 10 percent of the world's ocean is now inside a protected or conserved area — crossing the threshold for the first time in history.

The protected area total reached 10.01 percent as of June 8, 2026, after roughly five million square kilometres of new protections were established globally over the preceding two years — including 284 new marine and coastal protected areas across Indonesia and Thailand, and Ghana's first marine protected area at Greater Cape Three Points, declared after fifteen years of community advocacy. The milestone represents one-third of the way to the 30×30 target — 30% of oceans protected by 2030 — that governments committed to in December 2022, and coincides with the first full year of the UN High Seas Treaty in force. Scientists note that reaching 30% will require protecting the open ocean, not only near-shore waters.

Science & Medicine · Glasgow, Scotland

A phase 3 trial finds that finerenone slows kidney decline by 23 percent in patients with chronic kidney disease who do not have diabetes — the first treatment result for the most common form of the condition.

The FIND-CKD trial enrolled 1,584 patients with non-diabetic chronic kidney disease — including those with hypertension-related nephropathy and glomerular disorders — and found that finerenone reduced the composite endpoint of kidney failure, severe eGFR decline, and cardiovascular events by 23 percent relative to placebo over 32 months. Non-diabetic CKD accounts for the majority of cases globally but until now had no disease-modifying drug; FIND-CKD is the first large phase 3 study to fill that gap. Results were simultaneously published in the New England Journal of Medicine, The Lancet, and JAMA on June 5 and presented at the European Renal Association Congress in Glasgow.

Nature · Southern Bahia, Brazil

Brazil's national development bank approved R$87.2 million to replant 1,300 hectares of Atlantic Forest in Bahia using two million native tree seedlings from local nurseries.

BNDES approved the climate-fund financing on June 5 for the Muçununga Project — named after a coastal-forest ecosystem found only in this biome — channelling funds through Biomas and Carbon2Nature Brasil to plant native seedlings of more than 60 species across eight municipalities in southern Bahia, with income-generation programmes for 14 surrounding communities. The Atlantic Forest has lost more than 85 percent of its original cover; 2025 marked its lowest recorded annual deforestation since satellite monitoring began in 1985. The Muçununga Project is the largest single native-species restoration commitment in the biome this year.

Small Wonders · Bristol Bay, Alaska, USA

Beluga whales in Bristol Bay routinely change mating partners across breeding seasons — and a 13-year DNA study confirms the strategy is what keeps a small, isolated population genetically healthy.

Researchers at Florida Atlantic University analysed DNA from 623 beluga whales in Bristol Bay over thirteen years and found that both males and females consistently switched partners from one breeding season to the next throughout their reproductive lifetimes, rather than forming stable pair bonds. The flexible mating system maintains robust genetic diversity in a population of roughly 2,000 animals — effectively preventing inbreeding without any apparent coordination mechanism. Published in Frontiers in Marine Science on June 3, the study is the first to document serial partner variation in a small, geographically isolated cetacean population.

Quiet Kindness · Fuchu, Hiroshima Prefecture, Japan

A volunteer walking her poodle on an evening neighbourhood patrol in Hiroshima helped guide a disoriented elderly woman home — with the dog's calm presence providing the first moment of reassurance.

Eri Toya, a member of Fuchu Town's Wanpato Squad neighbourhood patrol in Hiroshima Prefecture, was out walking her medium poodle Rokuta when she noticed an elderly woman in pyjamas standing by the roadside, unable to state her name or address. Rokuta approached quietly; the woman visibly settled — having once kept a dog herself — and Toya was able to escort her toward the nearest police box, where they encountered the family already searching for her. Toya and Rokuta received a formal letter of appreciation from local authorities; Toya's response: "What makes me happiest is knowing that simply walking with Rokuta allowed us to contribute to the community."

Nature · Kimboza and Ruvu forest reserves, Tanzania

Trade bans and a decade of community forest restoration have brought Tanzania's turquoise dwarf gecko back from near-extinction — populations have returned to pre-collection levels for the first time since the illegal pet trade peaked.

The William's dwarf gecko, found only in Tanzania's Kimboza and Ruvu forest reserves across 34 square kilometres, was nearly wiped out when 40,000 specimens were captured for the global pet trade between 2004 and 2009. A CITES Appendix I listing and years of community-led reforestation cut fire frequency by 80 percent and removed nearly 100,000 invasive trees.

Small Wonders · Hanover, New Hampshire, USA

Octopuses have learned to use a mirror to locate prey hidden outside their line of sight — the first invertebrate shown to navigate by its own reflection, bridging 500 million years of separate evolution.

Dartmouth researchers trained three California two-spot octopuses to find food using a mirror mounted at the tank's edge; the animals succeeded roughly 73 percent of the time, turning 180 degrees toward their own reflection to reach prey they could not see directly.

Science & Medicine · Cambridge and Southampton, United Kingdom

A vaccine designed entirely by computer simulations passed its first human trial safely — triggering immune responses against SARS-CoV-2, SARS-CoV-1, and related bat-origin viruses in all 39 volunteers.

Scientists at Cambridge and Southampton tested pEVAC-PS in 39 healthy adults aged 18–50, confirming safety with no significant adverse events and broad immune responses across the sarbecovirus family. Cambridge spin-out DIOSynVax plans a Phase II trial in at-risk groups later in 2026.
