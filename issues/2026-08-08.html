Nature · Ile-Balkhash Nature Reserve, Kazakhstan

Kazakhstan released a tiger into the wild for the first time in more than 70 years.

On July 31, wildlife officials released a female Amur tiger named Ümit, Kazakh for "hope," into the Ile-Balkhash State Nature Reserve in the country's southeast, ending a 70-year absence since the native Caspian tiger disappeared. The reserve spent a decade rebuilding the ecosystem first, restoring populations of Bukhara deer, saiga antelope and kulan before officials brought in four tigers from Russia's Khabarovsk region this past May. Ümit now roams freely wearing a satellite collar, while the other three, including two cubs, remain in large adaptation enclosures and are expected to join her within the reserve in the coming years.

Science & Medicine · Southampton, England

A vaccine that needs no refrigeration passed its first human trial.

Researchers at the University of Southampton tested SPVX02, a tetanus-diphtheria booster that stays potent for at least two years at temperatures up to 30°C, in 60 healthy adults. By day 28, every participant who received it had reached protective antibody levels matching those from standard refrigerated vaccines, with only mild, expected side effects such as soreness and headache. Because roughly half the world's vaccines are wasted to breaks in the cold chain, developers hope a larger phase 2b trial will bring the shot closer to use in places without reliable refrigeration.

Global Progress · Gökova Bay, Turkey

Local fishers and rangers tripled Turkey's protected coastal waters.

Conservationists led by Zafer Kızılkaya worked with fishing communities along 310 miles of Turkey's Mediterranean coast to create or expand 18 no-fishing zones between 2019 and 2023, tripling the country's total protected seascapes. The Mediterranean Conservation Society trained local marine rangers to patrol the zones, and illegal fishing inside them has since dropped by 80 percent while more than 30 Mediterranean monk seals have returned to the area. Fishers working just outside the reserves report bigger catches too, a result organizers now use to bring skeptical neighboring villages on board.

Quiet Kindness · Zakariyapura, Gujarat, India

One woman's idea now turns cow dung into cooking fuel for 368 households.

After visiting a biogas plant in a neighboring village, Madhuben helped bring the technology to Zakariyapura, where cattle-owning families now convert dung and water into cooking gas at home. The project, backed by the National Dairy Development Board, grew into what organizers call the country's first all-women biogas cooperative, with members earning 3,000 to 5,000 rupees a month selling the leftover slurry as fertilizer. Households have cut spending on firewood and gas cylinders, kitchen smoke has dropped, and girls have more time for school now that gathering fuel takes less of the day.

Small Wonders · Tasmania, Australia

A thumb-sized fish that walks instead of swims became a photography milestone.

A red handfish, one of fewer than 250 left in the wild off Tasmania's southeast coast, became the 18,000th species photographed for National Geographic's Photo Ark. Photographer Joel Sartore captured the critically endangered fish at the University of Tasmania's marine institute, where researchers run a captive breeding program; rather than swim, it walks across the seafloor on modified fins. The milestone falls in the Photo Ark's 20th anniversary year, a project Sartore started to win affection for species too small or strange to otherwise get noticed.

Everyday Joy · Heather, Leicestershire, England

A Leicestershire village has covered itself in scarecrows every August for 28 years.

The Heather Scarecrow Festival returned to the small village of Heather for its 28th year, with homes, schools and shopfronts building scarecrow displays across the village from August 2 to 8. The entirely volunteer-run event, founded by Di Everett, sells £3 trail guides to visitors and raised more than £9,000 for local groups last year; the reigning champion entry was titled Ratacrowie. In 2025 the festival won the King's Award for Voluntary Service, the volunteer equivalent of an MBE, and thousands still turn up each summer to see what the village has built.

Nature · Okefenokee Swamp, Georgia

The Okefenokee Swamp became the first new natural UNESCO World Heritage site in the US in three decades.

The World Heritage Committee inscribed the Okefenokee National Wildlife Refuge on August 4, making it the 27th US site on the list and ending a fight over a proposed titanium mine on its border.

Global Progress · Oromia, Ethiopia

Ethiopia became the first country to have verified forest-carbon credits issued under the World Bank's newest standard.

The World Bank confirmed 14.9 million tonnes of verified emission reductions from Ethiopia's Oromia region, unlocking up to $372 million for the forest communities who protected it.

Science & Medicine · New Zealand

A trial of nearly 4,000 babies found paracetamol and ibuprofen are equally safe in infancy.

A trial of nearly 4,000 babies at the University of Auckland found paracetamol and ibuprofen equally safe for fever and pain in infancy, easing years of parental uncertainty.
