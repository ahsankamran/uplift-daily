Nature · Global / Southeast Asia

A study of four decades of satellite imagery finds that global mangrove forests have recovered to within 1 per cent of their 1980s extent, with net growth outpacing loss every year since 2010.

Researchers at Tulane University analysed Landsat imagery from 1984 to 2023 and found that after losing nearly 3,000 square kilometres between the 1980s and 2010, the world's coastal forests reversed course; by 2023, closed-canopy coverage had climbed from roughly 50 per cent to 58 per cent, and total net loss over the four-decade study period stood at less than 1 per cent. The paper, published in Science (vol. 392, June 2026), credits natural regeneration into newly available intertidal areas, the phase-out of destructive shrimp-pond conversion, and improved national management — particularly in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Myanmar, which posted the largest individual gains. Mangroves store up to five times as much carbon per hectare as tropical rainforest and shelter hundreds of millions of people from storm surges; the findings give conservationists a documented recovery to defend rather than only a decline to arrest.

Small Wonders · Cooktown, Queensland, Australia

Researchers in Queensland have documented a spider that builds a spring-loaded silk catapult to fling attacking ants more than 30 centimetres into its own web.

A newly described species of the genus Propostira, found in the rainforest near Cooktown in far north Queensland, weaves a cone of 15 to 60 tensioned silk strands that fires when a green tree ant bites the base — accelerating the ant at more than 1,300 metres per second squared, faster than a Formula One car's initial launch. The research, published 26 June in Current Biology by Professor Ajay Narendra's group at Macquarie University, found that the spider hunts only this single ant species, exploiting the moment the ants try to defend their own colony. Researchers have named the mechanism a "ballista" after the Roman siege weapon; it is the first known silk-based mechanical catapult in nature.

Quiet Kindness · Dartford, Kent, United Kingdom

A Polish-born woman in Dartford, Kent, who won £1 million gave her neighbours of 14 years £5,000 the same afternoon so they could take their first family holiday abroad.

Honorata Jamrozik, 48, had just returned from her father's funeral in Poland when she discovered on June 28 that she had won the UK Omaze Monthly Millionaire Draw; within hours she knocked on the door next to hers and handed over enough for the family beside her to book their first foreign trip together. Jamrozik, who came to the UK from Poland 25 years ago and has lived in Dartford throughout, said she had always wanted to do something like this for neighbours who had been quietly consistent throughout her years in the country. She plans to use the rest of the prize to resume fostering children — a vocation she set aside seven years ago to care for elderly parents and intends now to take back up.

Everyday Joy · Antarctica / United Kingdom

The BBC World Service transmitted its 71st annual Antarctic Midwinter Broadcast on June 21, carrying music requests and messages from home to the small crews spending the polar night at Britain's remotest research stations.

On the winter solstice, shortwave transmitters in Woofferton, England, and on Ascension Island in the South Atlantic beamed a thirty-minute programme to the overwintering scientists at Rothera, Bird Island, and King Edward Point — three British Antarctic Survey bases where fewer than 60 people in total are spending the polar winter. The broadcast followed the same format as the first transmission in 1955: music chosen by the station crews, messages from family and friends, and a word from the King; thousands of shortwave enthusiasts around the world tuned in and made recordings. Listener records from the SWLing Post, which has published recordings of the broadcast for fifteen years, noted this year's signal was notably clear across western Europe.

Science & Medicine · Calgary, Canada

A Phase I/II trial in Calgary found that 82 per cent of glioblastoma patients showed no disease progression at six months when high-dose vitamin B3 was added to standard treatment.

In the trial at the University of Calgary, the first 24 participants with newly diagnosed glioblastoma received controlled-release high-dose niacin alongside standard radiotherapy and temozolomide chemotherapy; 82 per cent showed no progression at six months, compared with a historical benchmark of roughly 54 per cent. Researchers found that high-dose niacin restored tumour-infiltrating immune-cell function and shifted the inflammatory environment within the tumour in an antitumour direction — the vitamin acting as a precursor to NAD+, which controls both energy metabolism and immune signalling. The results, published in the Journal of Neuro-Oncology (vol. 176(1), 2026), are now moving into a larger enrolment phase through early 2027, with the ambition of confirming whether a generic supplement costing cents per day can become a widely accessible immune adjuvant for one of the deadliest brain cancers.

Global Progress · Global

Global AIDS-related deaths have fallen 56 per cent since 2010, and United Nations member states adopted a declaration on June 23 committing to put 40 million people on antiretroviral treatment by 2030.

At the UN High-Level Meeting on HIV/AIDS in New York, an overwhelming majority of member states signed a Political Declaration anchored in documented progress: 32.1 million people are now on sustained antiretroviral therapy — the highest in the epidemic's history — alongside a 43 per cent drop in new global infections since 2010. The declaration sets a 2030 target of 40 million people on virally suppressive treatment and 20 million more reached by prevention tools including the twice-yearly injectable lenacapavir, currently in rollout in South Africa. UNAIDS described the declaration as the most consequential global AIDS agreement since the early-2000s access negotiations that first brought treatment within reach of lower-income countries.

Saturday · June 27
Nature · Pacific, Caribbean, Indo-Pacific

Researchers have identified 165,000 square kilometres of coral reef that may withstand climate change through 2050 — a new map of what to protect most urgently.

A study combining more than 45,000 field observations with AI climate modelling identified reef systems across 71 countries likely to remain functional through mid-century; the resilient areas are concentrated most densely in the Bahamas, Cuba, Australia, Indonesia, and the Philippines.

Small Wonders · North Island, New Zealand

A New Zealand cave has yielded the bones of a flying ancestor of the kākāpō, sealed in volcanic ash for a million years and never before seen in the fossil record.

Researchers from Flinders University identified a parrot ancestral to the kākāpō that was still capable of flight — revealing that the world's heaviest parrot lost its wings relatively recently in evolutionary time; the cave held 16 additional species not previously documented in New Zealand's fossil record.

Global Progress · South Africa

South Africa has begun rolling out lenacapavir — a twice-yearly HIV prevention injection — across 360 public health facilities in 24 high-burden districts, the world's largest deployment of this technology so far.

The rollout targets populations for whom daily pills have been hardest to sustain; the programme aims to reach one million people by the end of 2027 and three million within three years, in what health officials are calling the most consequential shift in HIV prevention since the introduction of daily PrEP.
