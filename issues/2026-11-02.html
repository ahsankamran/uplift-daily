Global Progress · Coffs Harbour hinterland, New South Wales, Australia

New South Wales has turned 178,000 hectares of state forest into a koala park.

The state parliament passed the bill creating the Great Koala National Park on October 23, folding 178,000 hectares of state forest and other public land into reserves already standing in the hinterland behind Coffs Harbour, for a park of about 475,000 hectares. Most of that forest had been set aside for native timber harvesting; it holds roughly 12,000 koalas and more than a hundred other threatened species, among them southern greater gliders, glossy black cockatoos and rare spider orchids. The government has put A$140 million towards establishing the park, which is due to open before the end of the year, and the National Parks Association of New South Wales — which ran the ten-year campaign for it — is already making the case for two excluded state forests to be added.

Nature · Alvarado Lagoons, Veracruz, Mexico

Mexican biologists are growing mangroves on mounds borrowed from Aztec farmers.

In the Alvarado Lagoons of Veracruz, the Mexican nonprofit REVIVE plants mangrove seedlings on chinampas — raised mounds of earth of the kind Aztec farmers built — so that young trees sit above water that would otherwise drown them. The biologist Aníbal Ramírez Soto turned to the method in 2006 after a first attempt at planting in rows failed; about 2,000 trees went into the first few hundred mounds, more than 4,000 more arrived on their own with the tides, and by year eight the canopy had closed over 80 percent of the site. The project now runs to some 15,000 mounds across 40 hectares, and the team has released about 80 Morelet's crocodiles in the belief that they keep channels open and shift sediment, an effect crocodile specialists call plausible but not yet demonstrated.

Small Wonders · Breekkrans River, Western Cape, South Africa

A critically endangered South African minnow now has ten kilometres of river instead of two.

The doring fiery redfin, a small fish of a few streams a couple of hours north of Cape Town, had been squeezed into a two-kilometre stretch of the Breekkrans River by spotted bass that anglers introduced in the late 1930s. Since 2023 the Freshwater Research Centre, CapeNature and the Fynbos Fish Trust have worked through each southern summer with nets, spearguns and electrofishers, clearing bass from eight kilometres of the upper river and building a barrier downstream to keep them out. The redfin's range has grown from two kilometres to ten, the river now holds the largest population left — probably a few hundred adults — and the team reports that every native fish in the cleared reach has gained ground.

Science & Medicine · 35 stroke centres, China

A clot can be pulled from the brain three days after a stroke, a Chinese trial finds.

The LATE-MT trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine, enrolled 336 adults at 35 Chinese stroke centres whose stroke came from a blocked large vessel, and gave half of them mechanical thrombectomy between 24 and 72 hours after symptoms began — a window usually treated as shut. At 90 days, 49 percent of the thrombectomy group were living independently against 35 percent of those on medication alone, and 26.9 percent had no significant disability against 15.6 percent. The procedure carries a real cost at that remove: bleeding in the brain affected 4.8 percent of treated patients and none of the others, and deaths ran at 6.0 percent against 1.8 percent, so the authors present the result as widening who might be considered rather than as routine practice.

Everyday Joy · City of Wanneroo, Perth, Western Australia

Perth residents spent their evenings in the bush listening for bats.

Researchers at Murdoch University handed ultrasonic detectors that clip onto a smartphone to a handful of curious volunteers in the City of Wanneroo, on Perth's northern edge, and sent them along set routes through local bushland after dark. Across 23 surveys the group recorded five species of microbat, Gould's wattled bat the most common among them, and picked up Holt's long-eared bat in Wanneroo for the first time. Questionnaires afterwards found the volunteers knew more about bats, felt more connected to the place they live, and simply liked the animals better than when they began.

Quiet Kindness · Pune, Maharashtra, India

Pune residents are measuring their city's riverbank trees one Saturday at a time.

Volunteers with Pune River Revival meet on Saturday mornings along the banks of the Mula and the Mutha with measuring tapes and phones, logging each tree's height, girth and species on iNaturalist; one recent outing was seven people working an assigned stretch. In a single month the group mapped more than 500 trees along one road and recorded 53 species among them, banyan and weeping fig and yellow flame. Pune's municipal tree census comes round only once every five years, so the volunteers fill the gap in between — Ananya Gandhi, who is 24, has mapped for two of those years and plans to keep contributing after she moves to Sweden to study.

Nature · Pemba Island, Zanzibar, Tanzania

Fishers on Pemba Island asked for marine reserves, and got 1,300 square kilometres.

About 1,300 square kilometres of reef, seagrass and fishing ground gazetted after fishers in Tumbe and twenty other villages wrote the rules themselves.

Small Wonders · Corvo, Azores, Portugal

An Atlantic island switched off its lights so young shearwaters could find the sea.

Four hundred and fifty residents darkened their streets for three weeks so fledgling shearwaters could steer by starlight instead of streetlamps.

Global Progress · Bridgetown, Barbados

Barbados has gone three years without a baby born with HIV.

WHO certification followed a review finding no infant born between 2022 and 2024 had acquired the virus from their mother.
