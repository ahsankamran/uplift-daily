Nature · Taro, Bali, Indonesia

A Balinese farmer is breeding fireflies back into the fields he grew up beside.

Wayan Wardika watched fireflies over his village's rice fields as a boy and then watched them go, thinned out by lighting, pesticides and building; since founding Bring Back the Light in 2020 he has released close to 20,000 larvae and adults, and the counts around Taro are climbing again. Lead scientist Cindy Chayani's team raises the larvae in purpose-built terrariums for up to two years and lets about 90 percent of the adults go, while Wardika composts manure from a facility that keeps Bali's sacred white cattle and brews a pesticide from fermented market waste. Breeding alone will not hold them, so he is trying to move 35 neighbouring farmers off agricultural chemicals — four so far, each given free compost and paid back for what the changeover costs them — because, as he puts it, if we don't see fireflies, there is something wrong with the ecosystem.

Small Wonders · Gainesville, Florida, USA

Seventy-seven kinds of hawkmoth answer a hunting bat by scrambling its sonar.

Paul Masonick of the Florida Museum of Natural History worked through 143 hawkmoth species in the dark, playing each one the calls of a bat and handling it as a bat would, and 77 of them replied with ultrasound loud enough to blur the hunter's picture of where they were. The sound comes from scales near the tip of the abdomen being scraped against the body, by three separate arrangements — one of them, a patch of minute bumps, had not been described before, and the species that use it make the widest range of noises. The work, published on November 4 in Proceedings of the Royal Society B, traces the trick to as many as seven separate inventions between 30 and 5 million years ago.

Quiet Kindness · Orangeville, Ontario, Canada

An Orangeville sewing circle is closing on its 45,000th handmade thing.

The Salvation Stitchers are about 20 people who meet twice a month in Orangeville, Ontario, and in roughly 15 years they have given away more than 40,000 items, with the running total now near 45,000. Betty McCutcheon began it as a small class for people who wanted to learn to sew; it now turns out chemotherapy caps, quilts, fidget blankets, tote bags, pencil cases, children's clothes and beds for animals, and several members stitch at home between meetings. The work goes to long-term care homes, hospices, shelters and food banks in the county, with a share passed on through Sew on Fire Ministries in Burlington.

Everyday Joy · Vatican City

A cleaning of the Sistine Chapel shows Michelangelo painted with brushes he made himself.

Restorers took three months to lift a whitish calcium-lactate bloom, left by the breath and sweat of visitors, off The Last Judgment with distilled water and sheets of Japanese tissue, the first cleaning of the altar wall in 30 years. Working that close, the Vatican's chief restorer Paolo Violini noticed that where one brushstroke wavered the strokes beside it wavered in exactly the same way, which can only happen if several tips are moving together: one brush had three, another was nearly six inches wide with ten. The arrangement copies the gradina, the many-toothed chisel Michelangelo used as a sculptor, carried across to a wall 45 feet high and more than 300 figures that took him from 1536 to 1541.

Science & Medicine · Perth, Western Australia

Lifting weights three times a week changed what people lost on a weight-loss drug.

In a 40-week randomised trial, 120 people aged 50 to 70 taking tirzepatide for weight management lost almost identical amounts — 22.3 kg with resistance training, 22.6 kg without — but the half who trained kept about a quarter more of their lean tissue. Strength moved in opposite directions: up roughly 10 percent, upper and lower body, in the group that trained three times a week, and down roughly 10 percent in the group that did not. Daniel Green at the University of Western Australia led the T-REx trial, published in Nature Medicine; everyone taking part got the same advice on protein, fibre and water, so the training is the difference.

Global Progress · Freetown, Sierra Leone

Sierra Leone will cook school lunch for 276,000 children this year.

The government has put SLE 158.4 million, about 6.6 million US dollars, through the education ministry into the National School Feeding Programme for 2026/27, after feeding more than 257,000 children in over 1,200 schools the year before. The food is bought at home — the launch of this year's round took in a million kilograms of Sierra Leonean rice — and the programme has already given steady custom to more than 22,000 smallholder farmers. By investing in school meals, Sierra Leone is investing in its children and its future, said Andrew Odero, the World Food Programme's country director.

Nature · Ascea, Campania, Italy

Loggerhead turtles keep nesting further west, and Italy's beach clubs are learning to share the sand.

Beach-club owners who once swept the tracks away now phone them in, and Ascea will require low-impact lighting on every concession from 2027.

Small Wonders · Leipzig, Germany

Thirty-one pet dogs have been taught to find invasive plants by smell.

Eleven of twelve volunteer teams passed the outdoor test, finding Himalayan balsam, ragweed and knotweed about 75 percent of the time.

Quiet Kindness · Berri, South Australia

A Riverland town cooks lunch together every week, and the group runs itself.

As many as 20 people cook a budget meal at the Berri Community Centre, often from produce picked in the centre's garden that morning.
