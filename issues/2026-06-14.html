Global Progress · Brazilian Amazon

Brazil's Amazon recorded just 3,182 square kilometres of deforestation alerts over the past twelve months — the lowest annual figure since 2014 and the lowest May reading in the history of satellite monitoring.

Brazil's National Institute for Space Research confirmed that cumulative Amazon deforestation alerts fell to their lowest 12-month level in over a decade, with May 2026 recording 370 square kilometres of clearing — a 61 percent drop from the 960 square kilometres tallied in May 2025. The decline holds across the two historically hardest-hit states: Pará dropped 52 percent and Mato Grosso 38 percent, driven by reinforced environmental enforcement and municipal cooperation programmes under the Amazon Fund, which has disbursed over R$5 billion in conservation payments since 2008. Environment Minister João Paulo Capobianco said if the trend holds through December, 2026 could mark the lowest annual deforestation rate since systematic satellite monitoring began in 1988.

Nature · Zinave National Park, Mozambique

Nine female white rhinos transported from South Africa have completed Mozambique's first viable rhino breeding population in nearly four decades at Zinave National Park.

The nine females arrived at Zinave on June 6 after a two-day road transfer from South Africa's Manketti Game Reserve — the longest-distance rhino translocation by road undertaken by Peace Parks Foundation to date. The park now holds 52 rhinos in total — 30 white and 22 black — following nearly a decade of reintroduction work by Peace Parks Foundation, Mozambique's National Administration for Conservation Areas, and Exxaro Resources; five black and two white rhino calves have already been born inside the park. Zinave is designed to serve as a source population for establishing rhinos across other protected areas in Mozambique, where the last wild individuals were lost to poaching in the 1980s.

Small Wonders · Vienna, Austria

Researchers analyzing 884 captive parrots across 30 species found that 413 birds use human names as specific labels for individual people and animals — not as random mimicry.

Scientists from the University of Vienna, University of Northern Colorado, and two partner institutions analyzed vocalizations from 884 parrots, finding that 413 birds used human names and at least 88 could be shown to apply a specific name to a specific individual rather than repeating it at random. One grey parrot said "goodnight" to each flock-mate by their correct name before settling for the night regardless of the order in which they were housed; at least ten birds called for absent people by name only when those people were not present in the room. The study, published in PLOS ONE, spans 30 parrot species and suggests the birds possess both the cognitive architecture and the vocal control to deploy a name as a deliberate social act — and to correct caregivers who used the wrong one.

Science & Medicine · Kyoto, Japan

A four-year Kyoto University study finds that older adults who continue playing a musical instrument preserve working memory and show measurably less brain shrinkage than those who stop.

Researchers followed 53 adults (average starting age 73) through four months of instrument training, then tracked their brains with MRI scans and verbal memory tests over four years; those who kept practicing maintained working-memory scores and showed significantly less shrinkage in the right putamen — a region critical to memory formation — while those who stopped showed measurable decline in both. The four-year design gave the study a clean internal comparison without requiring a separate non-music cohort, strengthening the causal reading. The findings, published in the peer-reviewed journal Imaging Neuroscience, suggest that sustained musical practice is a concrete and accessible route to slowing age-related brain change even when begun in one's early seventies.

Everyday Joy · George, Western Cape, South Africa

South Africa's 67 Blankets movement invited strangers to knit side-by-side at fifteen bookshops across three provinces, making squares and scarves for children's homes on Worldwide Knit in Public Day.

On June 13, the nonprofit 67 Blankets for Nelson Mandela Day partnered with Exclusive Books to host free crafting sessions at 15 stores across Gauteng, KwaZulu-Natal, and the Western Cape, providing needles, hooks, wool, and refreshments to anyone who walked in. In George, the local KnitWits group gathered at the Outeniqua Lifestyle Centre — one of hundreds of public meeting points the organisation uses to teach crafting to strangers in a single afternoon. The initiative began in 2013 with a challenge to honour Mandela's 67 years of public service with 67 handmade blankets; it now holds four Guinness World Records and counts active chapters in more than 50 countries.

Quiet Kindness · Western Highlands, Guatemala

Some 6,000 farming families across sixty villages in Guatemala's western highlands are sharing ancestral Maya techniques to make their own biopesticides, cutting household costs by five-sixths without outside instruction.

In sixty mountain communities, families are producing homemade biopesticides from chili, garlic, cinnamon, and endemic plants using knowledge passed down by grandparents and refined through informal local workshops — allowing households that once spent most of their income on agrochemicals to redirect savings toward school fees and food. The biopesticides cost one-sixth as much as commercial alternatives and reduced infestations by up to 90 percent in community trials, with techniques spreading across market days and neighbourhood visits without any formal programme. The revival follows decades in which industrial chemicals largely displaced traditional Maya agricultural knowledge, and farmers say the shift has restored a pattern of mutual reliance that younger generations had not seen before.

Global Progress · French Polynesia

French Polynesia has designated 520,000 square kilometres of ocean around the Austral and Marquesas Islands as the world's largest national marine protected area under the strictest conservation standard.

President Moetai Brotherson's IUCN Category 1 designation prohibits mining, trawling, and all industrial fishing across a zone roughly the size of France, with US$15 million in seed funding from the Te Moana Collective for a perpetual conservation trust.

Nature · Iberian Peninsula, Spain & Portugal

The Iberian lynx has reached 2,663 individuals — a 95 percent increase in four years and the highest count ever recorded — marking the first measurable step toward the population target for formal recovery.

The species now roams 26 distinct territories, up from fewer than 100 animals in 2002, with 542 breeding females and 952 cubs born in 2025 alone; the programme aims for 4,500 to 6,000 animals before requesting a formal downgrade from endangered to vulnerable on the IUCN Red List.

Everyday Joy · Spello, Umbria, Italy

For its 123rd year, some 2,000 residents of Spello spent the night of June 6 carpeting nearly two kilometres of medieval cobblestones with 1.5 million flower petals.

Roughly 70 teams of infioratori spent the night arranging flowers from 65 species across the Via Consolare without glue or paint — a tradition documented since 1581 — before 40,000 to 50,000 visitors arrived in the morning for the Corpus Domini procession.
