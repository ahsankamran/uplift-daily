Nature · Senegal, Madagascar, Chile, Canada

Six ocean sanctuaries across four continents have earned Blue Park status for the highest standard of marine protection — four of the awards going to sites in Africa.

At the Our Ocean conference in Mombasa on 16 June, the Marine Conservation Institute awarded 2026 Blue Park status to six protected areas: Kawawana, an Indigenous community conservancy in southern Senegal; three national parks in Madagascar including Nosy Hara, which shelters the island's largest sea-turtle nesting beach; Chile's Rapa Nui Marine Protected Area, spanning more than 700,000 square kilometres; and Canada's Banc-des-Américains, co-managed with the Mi'kmaq First Nations. Together the six sites add 455,000 square miles of well-managed ocean to the Blue Park network, now spanning 2.6 million square miles across 30 countries; all six were recognised for governance models that centre Indigenous peoples and local fishing communities, found to be more durable than protection imposed from outside. The Blue Park designation requires documented management effectiveness, not merely legal status — bringing the worldwide total to thirty-five certified Blue Parks.

Science & Medicine · England

In the five years to 2024, no woman in her early twenties has died from cervical cancer in England — the first time this has been true in recorded history.

A Lancet study published 22 June, analysing national mortality records from 2001 to 2024, found zero cervical cancer deaths among women aged 20–24 in the period 2020–2024 — the cohort offered the HPV vaccine at ages 12–13 when England's school programme launched in 2008. Without vaccination, researchers at Queen Mary University of London estimated approximately 23 deaths would have been expected in that group; across all vaccinated age cohorts, the programme has now prevented roughly 200 cervical cancer deaths in England. Lead researcher Peter Sasieni said that girls vaccinated at 12–13 now have "close to zero" risk of dying from cervical cancer before 30, with significant mortality reductions appearing in the 25–29 cohort as well — showing the protection advancing into older age groups year by year.

Global Progress · Italy

For the first time since the Middle Ages, Italy's forests cover more land than its farms — a reversal driven by young people returning to mountain villages.

A report published in June 2026 by Italy's National Union of Mountain Municipalities and Entities confirms that woodland now stretches across 60,000 square miles of the peninsula, surpassing farmland for the first time in roughly six centuries. The shift has brought bears and wolves back to mountain corridors; a single municipality — Marcetelli in Rieti Province — is now 94 percent forested, its trees providing carbon storage, water filtration, and erosion control valued at $9.5 million if replaced by industrial means. Since 2021, 932 Italian municipalities have recorded positive net migration, with young people returning to forested mountain areas — suggesting the land recovery and the human recovery are now reinforcing each other.

Small Wonders · Ealing, London, England

Five beavers introduced to a west London park in 2023 have quietly engineered the end of a decade of flooding at Greenford Underground station.

Within eleven months of arriving at Paradise Fields — a ten-hectare former golf course in the London Borough of Ealing — the beavers had dammed a creek, created a wetland, and stopped the chronic flooding that had repeatedly inundated Greenford station's ticket hall and surrounding streets. Four new species appeared in the first year (sticklebacks, dragonflies, damselflies, a migratory redpoll), the group has since grown to at least eight animals, and one kit holds the record as the heaviest beaver ever translocated in the UK. Everyday Joy · Neuchâtel, Switzerland

More than eleven thousand trains have rolled over a set of solar panels laid between live rails in Switzerland, and the first-year results are in.

Since installation in May 2025, a hundred-metre pilot on a regional line in Buttes, canton Neuchâtel, has generated 16,000 kilowatt-hours — enough for three to four households — with no damage to track or panels; Sun-Ways found that each passing train's air displacement sweeps the panels clean, solving the dust problem that had bedevilled earlier solar-rail designs. A three-panel module can also be detached in about ten minutes for routine track maintenance. France's SNCF, Italy's RFI, South Korea, and an Indonesian partner have all signed cooperation agreements, and the company estimates that panels across Switzerland's entire rail network could eventually meet two percent of national electricity demand.

Quiet Kindness · White Rock, British Columbia, Canada

A stranger heard a scooter crash in a White Rock neighbourhood, came out with first aid, loaded the broken bike into his truck, and drove the rider home.

Jay Cammack's electric scooter front wheel snapped off mid-ride on a neighbourhood path, sending him to the ground; Tom Gass, who owns two local businesses, heard the impact from inside, came straight out with paper towels, helped Cammack settle, then put the broken scooter in his truck and drove him home — the two having never met before that moment. Cammack later wrote a public thank-you letter noting that a helmet made the difference between a bruised afternoon and something far worse, and that Gass's unhesitating response made the recovery quick.

Small Wonders · Neotropical region

A Neotropical butterfly lives nearly three times longer than its closest relatives and shows almost no physiological decline as it ages — a discovery that may point toward the molecular roots of healthy longevity.

Researchers at the University of Bristol found that Heliconius hewitsoni lives up to 348 days while the closely related Dione juno survives only 14 — a 25-fold difference in lifespan; in at least one species, grip strength did not decline in older individuals. The study, published in Nature Communications, found pollen-feeding contributes to the advantage, but removing pollen from the diet did not eliminate it, pointing to multiple longevity mechanisms.

Science & Medicine · Leiden, Netherlands

A study of 212 long-lived families from Leiden has identified twelve rare genetic variants linked to healthy aging — including one mutation that appears to dial down inflammation while leaving immune function intact.

Researchers from Leiden University Medical Center found that middle-aged people with long-lived parents developed cardiometabolic diseases an average of thirteen years later than their partners. Scanning the genomes of 212 families, the team narrowed 20,000 candidate genes to just twelve rare protein-altering variants; one in the CGAS gene, governing inflammation, will now be tested in killifish at the Max Planck Institute in Cologne.

Global Progress · Bogotá, Colombia

Colombia's Congress has passed Latin America's first law specifically outlawing female genital mutilation — a milestone reached after two years of advocacy led largely by indigenous Emberá women.

The Senate approved Bill 440 unanimously on 10 June 2026, creating a framework for prevention, response, and eradication of FGM in communities where it has persisted in Chocó and Risaralda. The bill's authors embedded an "intercultural approach" — built through dialogue with Emberá communities — after years of advocacy by indigenous women and survivors who pressed the state to recognise FGM as a human rights violation.
