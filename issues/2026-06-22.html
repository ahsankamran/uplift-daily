Nature · Otago, New Zealand

Five strict no-take marine reserves protecting 308 square kilometres take effect on July 1 along New Zealand's Otago coast — the country's largest single expansion of marine protection in more than a decade.

Named Te Au Roa o Te Rakihouia — "the great current of Te Rakihouia," honouring an ancestor associated with the coastal waters of the lower South Island — the five reserves enclose towering kelp canopy, tidal reef, deep-water bryozoan thicket beds, and submarine canyon habitat in an area covering roughly four percent of the Otago coastal marine area. The reserves were gazetted on 28 May 2026 after more than a decade of consultation between the Department of Conservation, Kāi Tahu iwi, and fishing interests; under a legally binding co-governance protocol, a joint team of DOC rangers and Kāi Tahu kaitiaki will share day-to-day management with equal say on access, research, and enforcement — the first such arrangement for a New Zealand marine reserve. Species that stand to benefit most include hoiho (yellow-eyed penguins), which forage along the kelp margins; Hector's dolphins, the world's smallest known cetacean; New Zealand sea lions; and northern royal albatrosses, which nest on the Otago Peninsula and range across the reserve areas.

Small Wonders · Ceiriog Valley, Wales

Britain's longest dragonfly landed on conservation officer Sarah Hawkes' thumb during an early-morning walk beside a Welsh river — still too cool to fly, it simply rested on her hand.

Walking her dog before dawn along the Ceiriog River in the Berwyn Hills, Buglife Wales officer Sarah Hawkes felt a gentle touchdown and looked down to find a golden-ringed dragonfly (Cordulegaster boltonii) — Britain's longest insect, at nine centimetres — perched still, its gold-banded abdomen extended and wings spread flat. Dragonflies depend entirely on external warmth to fly, and in the cool morning air the insect was lethargic, giving Hawkes nearly a minute to photograph it at close range on her phone before it lifted away in the warming light. The Ceiriog catchment is designated as one of Britain's Important Insect Areas, recognised for the unusual clarity of its upland streams — the habitat the golden-ringed dragonfly needs to complete a four-year aquatic larval stage before it ever takes to the air.

Science & Medicine · 14 countries

The world's largest clinical trial for golden staph bloodstream infections found that two older antibiotics each cut mortality compared with the drug that has been standard of care for decades.

The SNAP trial enrolled patients at more than 150 hospitals in 14 countries and published simultaneous results in The Lancet and the New England Journal of Medicine in June 2026, finding that benzylpenicillin — first deployed in the 1940s — reduced 90-day mortality from 22 percent to 14 percent compared with flucloxacillin, the drug that has dominated treatment for decades. A second arm confirmed that cefazolin also outperformed flucloxacillin, and rates of acute kidney injury fell from roughly 22 percent to 11 percent. Both alternatives are older, cheaper, and more widely available than flucloxacillin in lower-income hospital systems, making the findings immediately actionable for the roughly 170,000 patients treated for golden staph bacteraemia each year.

Global Progress · Mombasa, Kenya

Fifteen countries signed the Mombasa Declaration on June 17, pledging to publish fishing-vessel registries and share catch data in the most concrete international step yet against illegal fishing.

At the 11th Our Ocean Conference in Mombasa, signatories including Ghana, Cameroon, Chile, Peru, South Korea, and France committed to publishing fishing licences, quota allocations, and vessel access agreements and assigning unique identifiers to every industrial boat in their fleets. Illegal, unreported, and unregulated fishing costs an estimated USD 50 billion annually and in West Africa — where up to 60 percent of animal protein comes from fish — directly strips coastal communities of food security and livelihoods. The declaration builds on the Global Charter for Fisheries Transparency, with an enforcement review to be held at the next Our Ocean Conference.

Quiet Kindness · Agra, India

Every afternoon since mid-April, Suyash Jain has walked a route through Agra to distribute chilled sharbat to outdoor workers — and his family tends fifteen bird-water stations across the city each morning.

During his 12–4 pm shift, seven days a week, Jain serves cold drinks to roughly 30–40 rickshaw pullers, construction workers, and street vendors — spending about 350 rupees a day of his own money on sugar, concentrates, and ice — while the family fills bird-water-and-feed stations placed on walls and railings across Agra before the working day begins. He traces the practice to his father, who has distributed grain to birds from his doorstep every morning for as long as his son can remember, and says the afternoon round now feels less like charity than the natural shape of the day.

Everyday Joy · London, UK

Five Guinness World Records for penny-farthing bicycles were broken in the City of London on June 13, including the world's largest — ridden on stilts — and the world's smallest, ridden by a six-year-old.

At the City of London Nocturne, Neil Laughton mounted "Big Bertha" using stilts — 282 centimetres tall with a 254-centimetre front wheel — to claim the record for the largest rideable penny farthing; siblings Flo (seven) and Finn (six) Woodward then took the women's and men's records for the world's smallest on "Little Bee," its front wheel just 29 centimetres across. Both machines were handcrafted by Australian specialist Dan Bolwell, who builds nothing but extreme penny farthings. Sweden's Henrik Hedström rounded out the evening by riding 100 metres while juggling in 19.87 seconds — the first formal measurement of the discipline, and a fifth world record for the afternoon.

Sunday · June 21
Nature · Kyrgyzstan

Kyrgyzstan formalized an 800,000-hectare wildlife corridor designed around 2070 climate models, giving snow leopards space to shift as mountain habitats change.

The Ak Ilbirs ecological corridor spans 14 rural municipalities and projects holding more than 60 percent of suitable habitat for snow leopards, argali sheep, and Asiatic ibex under future warming scenarios, while requiring seasonal grazing bans and a minimum 40 percent vegetation cover for wildlife movement, monitored by rangers and satellite tracking.

Global Progress · Atlantic Ocean

On World Sea Turtle Day, fifty organizations across 25 countries launched a coordinated alliance to protect Atlantic leatherback turtles, listed as critically endangered in part of their range.

The Wilkes Atlantic Leatherback Turtle Alliance (WALTA), led by University of Exeter, will commission a fresh nesting census across all Atlantic beaches and prioritize action at the beaches where the species faces the gravest decline, with the southwest Atlantic listed as critically endangered since the last formal population assessment in 2013.

Quiet Kindness · Orchid Island, Taiwan

Sixty Tao paddlers crossed 111 miles of open sea in a hand-built canoe to reunite with their Philippine kin after 300 years of silence.

On 15 June, a crew from Orchid Island navigated the Bashi Channel by stars through four-metre swells before reaching Batan Island on 17 June, where roughly 400 Ivatan residents gathered at the dock in a gesture of reconciliation answered with hours of shared drumming and the Tao war-canoe ceremony.
