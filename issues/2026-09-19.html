Small Wonders · Lampang, Thailand

Asian elephants showed they can stop and think before reaching for food.

At Thailand's National Elephant Institute in Lampang, researchers built a clear box holding fruit that elephants could only reach through a side opening, not by grabbing straight at what they could see. Every elephant tested used the trained opening instead of lunging for the visible fruit, and when researchers rotated the box, the animals paused and corrected course rather than reaching for its old position, according to Hunter College's Sydney Hope and CUNY's Joshua Plotnik, writing in PLOS One. It is the first documented evidence of this kind of self-control in elephants, and the team says it could help shape gentler ways for people and elephants to share the same land.

Global Progress · Athens, Greece

Athens is turning its old airport into one of Europe's largest parks.

The city is converting 400 acres of the former Ellinikon airport on its southern waterfront into a park second in size only to London's Richmond Park. Planners are putting in 30,000 trees and 3 million smaller plants, more than 500 species chosen to keep the grounds roughly 7 degrees Fahrenheit cooler than the surrounding asphalt. The park opens to the public in 2027, with further planting continuing through 2030 under Mayor Haris Doukas.

Nature · Mekong River, Cambodia

Six Irrawaddy dolphin calves were born in the Mekong this year.

Five calves surfaced in Cambodia's Kratie province and one in neighboring Stung Treng, lifting the river's known population of the endangered dolphins to about 118. Seventy-two river guards now patrol 180 kilometers of protected habitat, working with WWF-Cambodia to curb the illegal gillnets and electrofishing that helped cut the population from roughly 200 in the late 1990s. The dolphins have already vanished from the Mekong Delta and Tonle Sap Lake, so each calf gives the fishing communities whose tourism depends on them one more reason to keep the patrols funded.

Quiet Kindness · Codsall, England

A Staffordshire village started leaving anonymous notes of hope for strangers.

Ten collection boxes have gone up this month in Codsall, Staffordshire — at the village hall, a dentist's surgery, a florist and other stops — inviting residents to write a few lines of encouragement for someone they will never meet. Clinical psychologist Sophie Gwinnett designed the scheme, called Note from a Neighbour, with funding from South Staffordshire Council, so that from November anyone going through a hard patch can request a note without learning who sent it. Local A-level psychology students are helping run it, and Gwinnett hopes the low-cost idea catches on elsewhere if the pilot works.

Science & Medicine · Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA

Three students built a headset that screens for concussions in under a minute.

The device, called Rumble Rims, uses infrared eye-tracking to measure how well an athlete follows and fixates on a moving target, a sideline task that takes two to three minutes. Harvard students Radhika Saran, Cleo Pontone and Sophia DeVito designed it to close the gap between expensive clinical scanners and a coach's guesswork, and it won the James Dyson Award for the United States this year. About 15.1 percent of American high schoolers, roughly 2.5 million teenagers, report a sports-related concussion each year, and the team plans to use the $6,500 prize to refine the coach-facing display.

Everyday Joy · London, England

Nearly 1,200 Star Trek fans raised their hands together for a world record.

At London's Science Museum on September 10, 1,188 fans performed the split-fingered Vulcan salute to mark the franchise's 60th anniversary. Guinness World Records adjudicator Paulina Sapinska counted 934 people holding the gesture for a full minute, enough for a new record in a category that had never existed before, with actor Martin Quinn joining the crowd. Sapinska called it "a huge bonding moment," proof a fictional hand sign can still pull a room of strangers together.
