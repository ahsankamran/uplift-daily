Nature · Global

Earth's mangrove forests have turned a corner — for the first time in four decades, annual gains in coverage now outpace losses.

A study of four decades of satellite imagery, published in Science by Dr. Zhen Zhang and colleagues at Tulane University, found that global mangrove area — which fell nearly 2,900 square kilometres between the 1980s and 2010 — has been growing for the past sixteen years, driven by natural regeneration, conservation policy, and restoration in China, Australia, India, and the Americas; by 2023, the four-decade net loss had been reduced to just one percent, a far smaller figure than earlier models predicted. Existing mangrove forests are also becoming denser and healthier, improving their capacity to store carbon and buffer coastlines from storms. Co-author Daniel Friess of Tulane called the findings "a rare conservation success story," pointing to mangrove coastlines across more than 100 countries as viable targets for climate and biodiversity investment.

Everyday Joy · Altyn Dala, Kazakhstan

The last truly wild horses on Earth are galloping free on the Kazakh steppe again — five released onto the Altyn Dala reserve in the third wave of a transnational restoration.

Stallion Galvan from Prague Zoo and four mares from Hungary's Hortobágy National Park were released into the Altyn Dala State Nature Reserve on 25 May, following a year of supervised acclimatisation; eight more horses from Prague and Berlin arrived in early June. Przewalski's horses — the world's only undomesticated equine species, declared extinct in the wild by 1969 — are tracked via GPS collar as the new group explores pastures and watering holes across a protected steppe corridor of 40,000 square kilometres.

Global Progress · Tunis, Tunisia

Tunisia has become the 31st country to eliminate trachoma — ending the leading infectious cause of blindness after fifty years of sustained national effort.

The World Health Organization validated the achievement on 14 May 2026, recognising Tunisia as the 14th nation in the Eastern Mediterranean region to eliminate trachoma as a public health problem; the country's campaign began in the 1970s when nearly half of people in the southern regions were affected. Tunisia followed the WHO SAFE strategy — surgery for advanced scarring, antibiotics, facial cleanliness education, and improvements to water and sanitation — consistently over five decades.

Quiet Kindness · Dallas, Texas, USA

After a 2–2 draw with the Netherlands at the World Cup in Dallas, Japanese supporters stayed behind to collect cups and wrappers from their stadium section before leaving.

Fans in the Japan sections at AT&T Stadium picked up litter into bags on 14 June — a practice Japanese supporters have repeated at every World Cup since 1998 — while the Japan squad cleaned their locker room without being asked, confirmed by FIFA stadium staff. Nina Shimaguchi of the Japan American Society of Dallas-Fort Worth noted that Japanese schoolchildren clean their own classrooms and corridors from elementary age, with no custodians involved.

Small Wonders · Galápagos Islands, Ecuador

Scalloped hammerhead sharks — tracked by satellite on a 1,400-kilometre round trip from the Galápagos to Central American nursery waters — have won full international protection across their entire migratory range.

The Convention on the Conservation of Migratory Species granted the protections on 11 June, after Ecuador presented research showing pregnant females cross multiple national jurisdictions during their migrations, leaving them exposed in unprotected open ocean, while the global population has fallen more than 80 percent since the 1970s. Wildlife filmmaker Bertie Gregory called the ruling "a critical first step" and noted that enforcement by member states will determine whether populations have room to recover.

Science & Medicine · Berlin, Germany

A Berlin-led trial gave four in five patients with severe knee osteoarthritis meaningful pain relief through a minimally invasive procedure — no surgery, no stitches, and no general anaesthesia required.

In 194 adults whose knee pain had not responded to standard treatments, Dr. Florian Fleckenstein's team injected dissolving gelatin microspheres into abnormal blood vessels around the knee via a thin catheter, reducing inflammation; all 239 procedures were technically successful with no moderate or severe adverse events. The findings, published in Radiology, represent the largest body of evidence yet for genicular artery embolisation and may offer relief to the millions of patients globally who cannot safely undergo knee replacement surgery.

Nature · Austral & Marquesas Islands, French Polynesia

French Polynesia has placed 200,000 square miles of ocean surrounding the Austral and Marquesas Islands under full protection, sealing a decade of community planning into law.

The Te Tai Nui a Hau designation bans industrial fishing, deep-sea mining, and trawling across waters home to seabirds, sharks, whales, and species found nowhere else on Earth; combined with earlier protections, French Polynesia now safeguards roughly 540,000 square miles of ocean.

Everyday Joy · Gran, Hadeland, Norway

On a school field trip in Hadeland, six-year-old Henrik found a corroded iron blade half-hidden in the earth — a 1,300-year-old scramseax from the early Viking age.

Experts confirmed the single-sided iron knife likely belonged to a warrior in a region Norwegians call "warrior land"; the sword was transferred to Oslo's Museum of Cultural History. Henrik is six.

Small Wonders · Global

Scientists have mapped Earth's underground fungal highways for the first time and found 110 quadrillion kilometres of living thread — enough to circle the Sun a billion times — hidden beneath every field, meadow, and forest.

An international team used more than 16,000 soil cores to produce the first global atlas of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi; the networks carry an estimated 3.12 billion tonnes of CO₂ equivalent from plant roots into the soil each year.
