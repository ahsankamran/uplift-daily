Small Wonders · University of Bonn, Germany · November 4, 2026

Jumping spiders have two kinds of sleep, and in one of them their eyes move.

Daniela Rössler and Nadja Geiger at the University of Bonn filmed the jumping spider Portia fimbriata through the night: at sunset it spins a small silk platform, hangs upside down from it, grooms itself and drifts off, then cycles between a still, quiet state and a second one in which its legs curl, its body twitches and its eyes move. Spiders in that second state took an average of 4.27 seconds to respond to a loud noise, against 2.71 seconds when merely quiescent and 1.69 seconds when awake — the measurements were published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences on October 26. The line that led to spiders split from the line that led to us roughly 500 million years ago, and the Bonn group now wants to compare sleep across spider species, and in the wild rather than the lab.

University of Tübingen, Germany · November 3, 2026

Two crows called Iggy and Lemmy decide what they are looking at twice as fast as we do.

Andreas Nieder and Philipp Schmidbauer put two carrion crows in front of a touchscreen showing a cluster of dots, some drifting left or right and the rest moving at random, and the birds learned to tap the side matching the drift for a mealworm. Over thousands of trials, reported in Science on October 22, Iggy and Lemmy averaged 235 milliseconds a decision — roughly half the time monkeys and people take on comparable tasks, with no loss of accuracy — and electrodes picked up neurons in the nidopallium caudolaterale, the bird equivalent of a primate's prefrontal cortex, firing from 70 milliseconds after the dots appeared. Nieder reads it as evidence that quick, complicated computation needs no mammalian neocortex; whether other birds are as fast, nobody yet knows.

Breekkrans River, Western Cape, South Africa · November 2, 2026

A critically endangered South African minnow now has ten kilometres of river instead of two.

The doring fiery redfin, a small fish of a few streams a couple of hours north of Cape Town, had been squeezed into a two-kilometre stretch of the Breekkrans River by spotted bass that anglers introduced in the late 1930s. Since 2023 the Freshwater Research Centre, CapeNature and the Fynbos Fish Trust have worked through each southern summer with nets, spearguns and electrofishers, clearing bass from eight kilometres of the upper river and building a barrier downstream to keep them out. The redfin's range has grown from two kilometres to ten, the river now holds the largest population left — probably a few hundred adults — and the team reports that every native fish in the cleared reach has gained ground.

Corvo, Azores, Portugal · November 1, 2026

An Atlantic island switched off its lights so young shearwaters could find the sea.

Corvo, the smallest and most northerly island of the Azores, home to about 450 people, darkened its streets and public buildings from 21 October for close to three weeks. Fledgling Cory's shearwaters leave the burrow at night and steer by starlight, and the island's lamps had been drawing them down into the village instead; Corvo alone holds some 6,000 nesting pairs, part of the roughly three-quarters of the world's breeding population that nests in the Azores. SPEA, the Portuguese bird society, says the blackout has cut the number of birds found grounded or killed in collisions.

Seal Beach, California, USA · October 4, 2026

Two sea turtles nested on a Southern California beach, the first ever recorded on the U.S. West Coast.

On September 16 and 19, two olive ridley sea turtles came ashore in broad daylight at Seal Beach and Huntington Beach, California, and each dug a nest and laid eggs — the first sea turtle nests ever documented on the U.S. West Coast. NOAA and U.S. Fish and Wildlife staff fenced off the nests, then moved all 204 eggs to climate-controlled incubators at the Aquarium of the Pacific in Long Beach when storm surf threatened to wash them away. If the eggs hatch over the next two months, the hatchlings will be released on the same sand to start their lives as the first sea turtles ever born in California waters.

Queensland, Australia · September 30, 2026

Two stick insects new to science turned up in Australia, one hiding in plain sight for decades.

Researchers writing in Austral Entomology on September 21 described Anchiale mabiensis, whose lidded eggs carry concentric rings unlike any recorded in stick insects, and Anchiale robusta, misidentified for decades. They also reinstated Anchiale albopunctata, mistaken for another species for more than a century, and females of mabiensis reach 6.4 inches. Braxton Jones of the University of Sydney said even among Australia's most photographed insects, species are still escaping recognition.
