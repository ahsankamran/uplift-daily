Small Wonders · Seal Beach, California, USA · October 4, 2026

Two sea turtles nested on a Southern California beach, the first ever recorded on the U.S. West Coast.

On September 16 and 19, two olive ridley sea turtles came ashore in broad daylight at Seal Beach and Huntington Beach, California, and each dug a nest and laid eggs — the first sea turtle nests ever documented on the U.S. West Coast. NOAA and U.S. Fish and Wildlife staff fenced off the nests, then moved all 204 eggs to climate-controlled incubators at the Aquarium of the Pacific in Long Beach when storm surf threatened to wash them away. If the eggs hatch over the next two months, the hatchlings will be released on the same sand to start their lives as the first sea turtles ever born in California waters.

Queensland, Australia · September 30, 2026

Two stick insects new to science turned up in Australia, one hiding in plain sight for decades.

Researchers writing in Austral Entomology on September 21 described Anchiale mabiensis, whose lidded eggs carry concentric rings unlike any recorded in stick insects, and Anchiale robusta, misidentified for decades. They also reinstated Anchiale albopunctata, mistaken for another species for more than a century, and females of mabiensis reach 6.4 inches. Braxton Jones of the University of Sydney said even among Australia's most photographed insects, species are still escaping recognition.

Paris, France · September 29, 2026

A charred Gallic wall under a Paris hospital may mark the town Caesar besieged.

Archaeologists working in the Hôtel-Dieu courtyard on the Île de la Cité uncovered about 20 metres of a wall of stone and horizontal timbers, carbon-dated to between 200 and 10 BCE. Behind it lay a two-metre platform of sand and gravel, a Gallic spearhead and the remains of a burned wooden building, which fits Caesar's account of the Parisii burning their island town in 52 BCE. France's Ministry of Culture announced the find on September 23, and a cast of the wall will go to a museum because planned hospital works mean the original cannot stay.

Scotland · September 26, 2026

A gannet's mid-dive torpedo shape won the year's top bird photograph.

UK photographer Henley Spiers won the 2026 Bird Photographer of the Year grand prize for an image of a northern gannet folding into a torpedo shape as it pierced the ocean's surface off Scotland. Judges chose the shot from more than 24,000 entries submitted across eight categories, from owls in flight to a heron tangled in fishing line. Contest director Will Nicholls said every image in the competition represents "a real moment in the wild, earned through patience."

Durmitor and Komarnica Canyon, Montenegro · September 25, 2026

Scientists found two snails in Montenegro's mountains with shells that swing shut like doors.

Citizen scientists and researchers working in Durmitor National Park and Komarnica Canyon identified Cochlostoma komarnica and Cochlostoma dobrido, two cone-shelled snails barely six millimeters long that graze algae off limestone rocks. Unlike most land snails, they carry their eyes on the sides of their heads, and a disk on the tail seals the shell shut like a trapdoor when they retreat inside. The team, organized through Taxon Expeditions over eight years of fieldwork, says one species' only known home sits in a canyon threatened by a proposed hydropower dam.

Côte-Nord, Quebec, Canada · September 24, 2026

An amateur astronomer scouting a campsite found a 390-million-year-old crater instead.

While scanning satellite maps of Quebec's remote Côte-Nord region for a place to camp, Joël Lapointe noticed a strange 25-kilometer ring near Lake Marsal and flagged it to researchers. Fieldwork in 2025 turned up shatter cones confirming a meteorite strike some 390 million years ago, and the structure — now named Uhackatik with the Innu Council of Ekuanitshit — is the largest crater found since Greenland's Hiawatha structure in 2018. Lapointe had only been looking for a quiet spot to pitch a tent.
