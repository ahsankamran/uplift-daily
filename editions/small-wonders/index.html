Aberdeen, Scotland · November 18, 2026

Bumblebees turn out to have a preferred amount of salt in their nectar.

Tegan Gaetano and Lesley Lancaster at the University of Aberdeen offered buff-tailed bumblebees artificial nectar dosed with calcium, potassium, magnesium or sodium at a range of strengths, and the bees turned away from the strongest version of all four. What they settled on was a middling level of sodium, the first demonstration that a bumblebee has a preferred amount of salt rather than simply putting up with whatever it finds. The level they chose sits above the sodium most flowers actually supply, which the pair take in Royal Society Open Science as a sign that nectar alone may not give a bee everything it is after.

Ramat Gan, Israel · November 17, 2026

Two gorillas worked out that one of them could be a ladder.

At the Ramat Gan Safari outside Tel Aviv, Lisha braced herself on all fours on a rock and her half-sister Amelia climbed onto her back, stood upright and snapped off a branch 4.5 metres up that an electric barrier around the trunk had put out of reach. Michael Levitzky and Israel Hershkovitz of Tel Aviv University found three of these episodes on security footage — in 2021, 2024 and 2025 — with the pair taking the same roles each time, and in the 2025 clip Lisha walks over and positions herself without being pushed. The two normally sleep apart and rarely groom each other, and in 2025 Amelia ate the greenery alone, so the authors write in Primates that this kind of cooperation seems to need neither closeness nor a share of the spoils.

Southern Poland · November 15, 2026

Nineteen slow worms reported as vipers were lifted out of a water meter chamber.

A wildlife centre in southern Poland was called to an underground water meter chamber after someone reported venomous snakes inside, and found 19 slow worms — a legless lizard, harmless and often mistaken for one — sheltering from the cold. The chamber's smooth vertical walls had let them slip in and given them nothing to climb back out on, and because the animals had already dropped into winter torpor they could not simply be set down outside. Leśne Pogotowie took them to a rehabilitation shelter to hibernate at a steady low temperature, and will carry them back to the wild in spring.

Caribbean reefs · November 14, 2026

A tiny reef fish shares almost everything with the sponge it lives in.

Tusked gobies, among the smallest fish on Caribbean reefs, live in groups inside living sponges, and larger sponges hold larger groups arranged in a hierarchy by size. Theresa Rueger's team at Newcastle University, with colleagues in Belize and the United States, sequenced the fish and found no sign that sponge-mates are related — they are neighbours rather than family — and more than 500 kinds of bacteria shared between the gobies' guts and the sponges around them. The two papers, in The American Naturalist and Molecular Ecology, argue that where one of these fish settles goes on to shape nearly everything about it.
