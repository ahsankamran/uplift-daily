Small Wonders · University of Naples Federico II, Italy · November 11, 2026

An octopus gets through a hole in three moves, or four if it is tight.

Michael Kuba and colleagues in Naples, Vienna and Singapore measured the gap between each animal's eyes as a stand-in for the stiff cartilage case around its brain, then gave it acrylic partitions with holes from about 10 to 35 millimetres across. Where the hole was wider than that case, a common octopus posted one arm through to feel the far side, threaded the others after it, and hauled body and all through in a single pull; where it was narrower, the pull came in two parts, arms first and then the mantle squeezed down and dragged after. Mayan octopuses used the same routine on the roomier holes and left the tight ones alone, and the team, publishing in the Journal of Experimental Biology on November 5, wants the sequence for soft robots that have to work in narrow spaces.

Gainesville, Florida, USA · November 10, 2026

Seventy-seven kinds of hawkmoth answer a hunting bat by scrambling its sonar.

Paul Masonick of the Florida Museum of Natural History worked through 143 hawkmoth species in the dark, playing each one the calls of a bat and handling it as a bat would, and 77 of them replied with ultrasound loud enough to blur the hunter's picture of where they were. The sound comes from scales near the tip of the abdomen being scraped against the body, by three separate arrangements — one of them, a patch of minute bumps, had not been described before, and the species that use it make the widest range of noises. The work, published on November 4 in Proceedings of the Royal Society B, traces the trick to as many as seven separate inventions between 30 and 5 million years ago.

Leipzig, Germany · November 8, 2026

Thirty-one pet dogs have been taught to find invasive plants by smell.

Between 2021 and 2023 the Helmholtz Centre for Environmental Research in Leipzig trained 31 volunteers and their own dogs to detect Himalayan balsam, common ragweed and the knotweeds, three of the plants spreading fastest across Europe. Twelve teams sat the final test outdoors and eleven passed, finding the target plants about 75 percent of the time; Annegret Grimm-Seyfarth and colleagues published the results on November 4 in PLOS One. They also set down what such a scheme needs — how to choose handlers, how long to train, how closely to watch the first searches — because, as one of the authors put it, Enthusiasm is a great starting point, but reliable monitoring requires more than a good nose.

22 countries across Europe · November 7, 2026

Camera traps in 22 countries have made one shared album of Europe's wildlife.

Snapshot Europe has released about 100,000 observations of mammals and birds, gathered at more than 1,000 sites a year by over 160 camera-trap specialists from 118 universities, field stations, parks, museums and conservation groups. Fabiola Iannarilli of the Max Planck Institute of Animal Behavior and Francesca Cagnacci of Euromammals coordinated the survey, published on November 6 in Ecology; red deer were photographed most often, then roe deer, wild boar, red fox and European rabbit. Rabbits in Belgium turned up as often as ten times a day, the Casentino forests in Italy held the most species at fifteen, and every image is now public.

Eastern Sierra Nevada, California, USA · November 5, 2026

Ground squirrels with more female relatives nearby come through hibernation better.

Twenty-five years of marking and recapturing Belding's ground squirrels at a field site in California's eastern Sierra show that adult females survived the winter more often when more of their adult female kin were living close by. Jill Mateo and colleagues at the University of Chicago, writing in PLOS One on November 4, found no such effect for males or for young females, and recorded the heaviest losses among juvenile males. Shorter snow seasons brought the squirrels up out of their burrows earlier, which went with more pups surviving and more young per mother.

University of Bonn, Germany · November 4, 2026

Jumping spiders have two kinds of sleep, and in one of them their eyes move.

Daniela Rössler and Nadja Geiger at the University of Bonn filmed the jumping spider Portia fimbriata through the night: at sunset it spins a small silk platform, hangs upside down from it, grooms itself and drifts off, then cycles between a still, quiet state and a second one in which its legs curl, its body twitches and its eyes move. Spiders in that second state took an average of 4.27 seconds to respond to a loud noise, against 2.71 seconds when merely quiescent and 1.69 seconds when awake — the measurements were published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences on October 26. The line that led to spiders split from the line that led to us roughly 500 million years ago, and the Bonn group now wants to compare sleep across spider species, and in the wild rather than the lab.
