Nature · Tawi-Tawi, Philippines · November 7, 2026

Horseshoe crabs have been confirmed in a second Philippine province.

Researchers interviewed 142 residents across four municipalities of Tawi-Tawi and logged sightings in Bongao, Simunul, Sitangkai and Panglima Sugala, the first record of the animals anywhere in the Philippines outside Palawan. Ivane Pedrosa-Gerasmio of Mindanao State University–Iligan Institute of Technology first heard of them at a Bongao market in December 2023; residents call them bangkas, after the shape of a boat, and the two kinds described resemble the endangered tri-spine and the mangrove horseshoe crab. More than half of those interviewed believe numbers are falling, and her student Quesie Tolero Omnos argues the new record should guide what local councils choose to protect.

Islands off western Britain · November 5, 2026

Shearwaters flying home through the night drift off course, the way we get jet lag.

Twenty years of GPS tracks from 1,045 breeding Manx shearwaters — 3,688 trips in all, 1,837 of them homeward across the Irish Sea and the northeast Atlantic — show the birds veering clockwise by as much as 15 degrees when they fly in the dark. Patrick Lewin, an ecologist at the University of Oxford, and his colleagues report in Science that the drift looks like an internal clock briefly out of step with the sun, the same mismatch that leaves a long-haul passenger groggy. It costs the birds very little: on a homeward leg of roughly 1,000 kilometres, a seven-degree error adds about five.

Ile-Balkhash State Nature Reserve, Kazakhstan · November 4, 2026

A second tiger is loose in the Ili delta, where none has lived for 70 years.

Kazakhstan's ecology ministry let a male Amur tiger, named Amur, out into the Ile-Balkhash State Nature Reserve on October 31, three months after a female called Umit walked out of her enclosure on July 31. Both wear satellite collars; they came from a group of four tigers brought from Russia's Khabarovsk Territory in May, and the reserve has been laying in their food first — 30 kulans were moved across from Altyn-Emel national park in September, along with Bukhara deer. Two cubs are still under supervision and will go out when keepers judge them ready; the aim, set with the Amur Tiger Centre and WWF, is a population in the Ili delta and southern Balkhash that breeds without help.

River Ness catchment, Highland, Scotland · November 3, 2026

Beavers are going back into the waters around Loch Ness after 500 years.

NatureScot approved a licence on October 30 for Trees for Life and the Beaver Trust to release up to 15 beaver families into the River Ness catchment over three years, five centuries after the last beaver was recorded at the loch. The animals will come from farmland elsewhere in Scotland where they are damaging prime agricultural ground, be trapped under licence and health-screened before being moved; more than 73 percent of those who answered NatureScot's consultation supported the plan, and the agency says it is taking seriously the concerns raised by land managers, fisheries managers and people living in the catchment. The first releases could come within weeks, in a catchment NatureScot identified back in 2022 as one of the most suitable in Scotland.

Alvarado Lagoons, Veracruz, Mexico · November 2, 2026

Mexican biologists are growing mangroves on mounds borrowed from Aztec farmers.

In the Alvarado Lagoons of Veracruz, the Mexican nonprofit REVIVE plants mangrove seedlings on chinampas — raised mounds of earth of the kind Aztec farmers built — so that young trees sit above water that would otherwise drown them. The biologist Aníbal Ramírez Soto turned to the method in 2006 after a first attempt at planting in rows failed; about 2,000 trees went into the first few hundred mounds, more than 4,000 more arrived on their own with the tides, and by year eight the canopy had closed over 80 percent of the site. The project now runs to some 15,000 mounds across 40 hectares, and the team has released about 80 Morelet's crocodiles in the belief that they keep channels open and shift sediment, an effect crocodile specialists call plausible but not yet demonstrated.

Pemba Island, Zanzibar, Tanzania · November 1, 2026

Fishers on Pemba Island asked for marine reserves, and got 1,300 square kilometres.

Tanzania has gazetted the North-East and South-East Pemba conservation areas, about 1,300 square kilometres of reef, seagrass and fishing ground off Zanzibar's second island. The request came from fishers in Tumbe and more than twenty other villages who had watched their catches thin, and who then worked with the Department of Blue Economy and Fisheries, the Wildlife Conservation Society, Blue Alliance and Mwambao to write no-take zones, spawning closures and gear rules into village by-laws. Blue Alliance reports patrols up by more than half and illegal fishing down about 45 percent, and the next task is building income that does not depend on the net — sea cucumber farming, seaweed drying, tourism.
