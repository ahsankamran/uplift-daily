Quiet Kindness · Leith, Edinburgh, Scotland · November 13, 2026

Leith has bought its own church.

The Leith St Andrew's Trust has bought Leith St Andrew's church from the Church of Scotland with £589,750 from the Scottish Land Fund, which puts the building into the hands of the neighbourhood that uses it. The trust is run by volunteers and local members and had earlier help from the Architectural Heritage Fund, the National Lottery Heritage Fund and the Community Ownership Support Service at DTAS. The groups already meeting there keep their rooms, and the trust says the purchase is meant to secure the place as a vibrant hub for another 145 years.

Ashdon, Essex, England · November 12, 2026

Ashdon has a village shop again, ten years after the last one closed.

A small store opened on November 12 in the car park of the Rose and Crown in Ashdon, Essex, about ten years after the village's only shop shut. The pub's owners, Vicki and Dean Foster and Cleo and Iain Ford, built it with advice and a grant from Pub is The Hub, and stocked it with everyday groceries, fresh local produce and household goods, with coffee at the counter. Solar panels sit on the roof and there is a charging point outside, and Dean Foster said they were thrilled to bring Ashdon its village store once again.

Hobart, Tasmania, Australia · November 11, 2026

A Hobart scheme has been sending gardeners round to older neighbours for twenty years.

Still Gardening, run by the City of Hobart across Hobart and Glenorchy, pairs a volunteer with an older resident for an hour or two in the garden every fortnight, and has been doing it for close to 20 years. A new participant gets a group working bee at the house first, then one volunteer matched on shared interests and what the garden needs; some pairs have kept at it for a decade, and Lyn Kirkwood, 72, has been gardening with Lachlan Chisholm, 35, for six months. A further $400,000 from the Commonwealth Home Support Programme keeps it going two more years, and coordinator Monique Brumby is looking for more volunteers, including people who have never gardened.

Orangeville, Ontario, Canada · November 10, 2026

An Orangeville sewing circle is closing on its 45,000th handmade thing.

The Salvation Stitchers are about 20 people who meet twice a month in Orangeville, Ontario, and in roughly 15 years they have given away more than 40,000 items, with the running total now near 45,000. Betty McCutcheon began it as a small class for people who wanted to learn to sew; it now turns out chemotherapy caps, quilts, fidget blankets, tote bags, pencil cases, children's clothes and beds for animals, and several members stitch at home between meetings. The work goes to long-term care homes, hospices, shelters and food banks in the county, with a share passed on through Sew on Fire Ministries in Burlington.

Berri, South Australia · November 8, 2026

A Riverland town cooks lunch together every week, and the group runs itself.

Cooking Outside the Box meets weekly at the Berri Community Centre in South Australia's Riverland, where as many as 20 people cook a meal on a budget, often from fruit and vegetables picked in the centre's garden that morning. The not-for-profit ac.care set it up with Foodbank's help, though coordinator Sue Dayman says we don't really run it; basically, the group runs themselves — one member added a Zumba session to fill the time while the food is in the oven. Janet Becker, who joined a year ago, says it got her cooking at home again, and the group once made enough to feed itself and the people sleeping outside the centre.

Castlemilk, Glasgow, Scotland · November 7, 2026

Castlemilk has a shop selling fresh food again, and it is not there to make money.

Ardenglen Housing Association and Good Food Scotland have opened a not-for-profit community shop in the Glasgow neighbourhood, selling fresh produce and household essentials at up to 30 percent below the nearest supermarket. It replaces a pantry where residents paid a set price for about twelve items from a short list; Ardenglen's Suzanne Casey said that model was not sustainable in the long term, and residents helped shape what took its place. It is the tenth such shop Good Food Scotland runs in the city, between them serving more than 4,000 members, and its funding is secured only to the end of March.
