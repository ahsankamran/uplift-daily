Science & Medicine · Manchester, England · September 30, 2026

A simple cell test could spare many women with postmenopausal bleeding an invasive check for womb cancer.

The DETECT study tested urine and vaginal fluid from 1,864 women, 115 of whom had cancer, and the combined test found over 80 percent of cancers, including 95.8 percent of high-grade tumours. Its negative predictive value for womb cancer was 98.8 percent, so a clear result is very likely to be right. Professor Emma Davidson, who led the work, said further studies in routine practice are needed before it reaches clinics.

Seoul, South Korea · September 29, 2026

Blood thinners cut serious events by 69 percent in a middle-risk group of atrial fibrillation patients.

The SINGLE-AF trial followed 1,803 people with atrial fibrillation at intermediate stroke risk, comparing direct oral anticoagulants with no anticoagulation. Serious events, including stroke, major bleeding and cardiovascular death, occurred in 0.5 percent of those on the drugs against 1.5 percent of those without, and ischemic stroke in 0.1 percent against 1.1 percent. Professor Boyoung Joung of Yonsei University, who led the trial, said the New England Journal of Medicine results may inform guidelines and reimbursement.

Los Angeles, California, USA · September 26, 2026

A radiation-and-radioligand combo slowed prostate cancer's return.

In a UCLA-led study presented at the ASTRO 2026 meeting, men with recurrent prostate cancer who added targeted radioligand therapy to focused radiation went a median 19.4 months before their cancer progressed, compared with six months on radiation alone. Radiation oncologist Esther Yu and colleagues found a hormone-therapy combination performed similarly, at 21.7 months, but caused serious side effects in twice as many patients as the radioligand approach. The findings give doctors a way to delay the cancer's return while sparing some men the harsher effects of hormone treatment.

Manchester, England · September 25, 2026

A urine test found four in five womb cancers without a single invasive procedure.

University of Manchester researchers tested 1,864 women with postmenopausal bleeding using a new method that reads cells from urine and vaginal fluid, published this week in The Lancet Obstetrics, Gynaecology & Women's Health. The test correctly flagged more than 80 percent of the 99 confirmed womb cancers and cleared 93 percent of women who didn't have cancer, sparing many an anxious wait for a hospital biopsy. Lead researcher Professor Emma Davidson says the simple sample could let doctors reassure most women much earlier in the process.

Beijing, China · September 24, 2026

Nine years of wrist-tracker data linked deep, dreaming sleep to a lower risk of dozens of diseases.

Peking University researchers tracked 95,559 UK Biobank participants who wore wrist accelerometers for a week, then followed their health records for the next nine years. Led by Jingsong Luo and published this September in PLOS Medicine, the team found more REM sleep tied to a lower risk of 83 conditions including heart failure and dementia, while more deep sleep lowered the odds of seven others, including type 2 diabetes. The researchers say sleep architecture, not just how many hours you get, may be a simple signal doctors could watch for early risk.

Cambridge, England · September 23, 2026

Reading for pleasure lowers dementia risk and builds empathy, a review found.

University of Cambridge researchers Barbara Sahakian and Christelle Langley reviewed the evidence on reading and health, published September 15 in Psychological Medicine. A study following 5,500 adults over 55 for five years found frequent readers were significantly less likely to develop cognitive impairment, with reading linked to a 33 percent lower risk of Alzheimer's disease. Nearly two-thirds of readers said books helped them understand other people's feelings, compared with fewer than half of non-readers.
